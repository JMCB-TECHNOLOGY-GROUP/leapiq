01 — Scope

What “national” actually means

The platform today is a working demonstration: one class, one browser, data that lives on a single device. Nothing about it is wrong; everything about it is small. The gap between that and a national system is not features — it is scale, offline operation, identity, and assurance.

Guyana runs its schools through eleven education districts: the ten administrative regions plus Georgetown as District 11. Four of those regions are hinterland — Barima-Waini, Cuyuni-Mazaruni, Potaro-Siparuni and Upper Takutu-Upper Essequibo — and they set the hardest constraints on the design. A system that only works on the coast is not a national system.

1,033 schools have internet; roughly 120 do not. The Ministry and the NDMA have connected 1,033 schools — 385 nursery, 489 primary and every secondary school — including 365 hinterland and remote schools in the last two years, 158 of them over low-earth-orbit satellite. That is a strong base and it is not full coverage, and in many hinterland schools power is as intermittent as the link.

So the platform is designed offline-first, not offline-tolerant. A school with no connection for a fortnight loses nothing.

02 — Re-architecture

What has to change to run this nationally

Six things change. The pedagogy engine — ingest, baselines, plan, mastery — does not: it is already written as pure functions over plain data, which is exactly what lets it run unchanged on a teacher's tablet in Mahdia with no signal.

03 — The decision that sets the running cost

Generate the questions once, not two million times

The single largest lever on the annual bill is where the AI runs. Generating questions live, on demand, for every pupil in every session is the obvious design and it is the wrong one — at national scale it costs more than the entire rest of the platform combined, and it stops working the moment the link drops.

Generating the item bank in batch — once per academic year, per grade, per subject, per strand — puts a Guyanese teacher between the model and the child, ships the questions inside the offline content pack, and costs almost nothing to run.

US$967,000 a year, saved by a design choice rather than a discount. It also removes the dependency that would have made the platform useless in exactly the schools that need it most.

05 — Rollout

Four phases in thirty months, national primary in twelve

Every phase still ends with a result the Ministry can check independently, and none of them commits the country to the next. The compression comes from running things in parallel, not from cutting them: the pilot widens instead of lengthening, district training waves run four at a time instead of two, and handover starts a year earlier.

Foundation

Months 1–2 · compressed from 3

Curriculum digitisation with NCERD, so every strand and objective in the platform is the Ministry's own. Data-sharing agreement and data-protection impact assessment signed before a single pupil record moves. School census extract loaded. Compressed by running the legal track and the curriculum track concurrently rather than in sequence — the DPIA does not depend on the strand mapping.

Months 3–7 · Grades 4–6 · compressed from 6 months to 5

The measure is unchanged: whether plan-driven teaching moves Grade 6 strand mastery against a matched comparison group, and whether the offline client survives real hinterland conditions for a full term.

Teacher training is the rollout

Ten and a half thousand teachers cannot be trained centrally, and the platform is worthless to a teacher who does not trust it. Training runs as a cascade: master trainers drawn from serving Guyanese teachers, then a lead in each school, then everyone. Hinterland districts get in-person delivery and a travel budget that reflects what it actually costs to reach Regions 1, 8 and 9 — that premium is the largest single line in the rollout budget and it is not negotiable.

Acceleration hits this line hardest. Running four district waves at once means 120 master trainers instead of 60, and a trainer who would have covered three districts in sequence now covers one. That is the whole of the +US$80,000 on the training line: the same teachers trained, by more people, in less time.

Pupil-years are the average of start- and end-of-year coverage, summed across the five years. It is the fairest single comparator because it prices the calendar as well as the cash: a plan that reaches a child in year 1 buys four years of that child's schooling, a plan that reaches them in year 3 buys two.

Where the money comes from

Nothing is cut. The acceleration is funded by ending programme-level delivery twelve months earlier: handover starts at month 22 instead of month 34, so years 4 and 5 fall from US$1.69M and US$1.45M to US$784,000 and US$582,000. That releases US$1.77M, which pays for the bigger year-1 and year-2 teams.

Two lines go up and stay up. Training rises US$80,000 because parallel waves cannot reuse a trainer across districts. Technology rises US$78,000 because full-scale hosting, AI item banks and the annual penetration test all arrive a year sooner. Both are paid for out of the same US$140,000 that comes off the People line.

06 — Proportion

What this costs against what Guyana already spends

Budget 2026 allocates GY$183.6 billion to education. At its most expensive year this programme is now GY$481 million — just over a quarter of one per cent of a single year's education spending. The acceleration raises the peak-year call on the budget from 0.19% to 0.26%, and in exchange puts a national instrument for seeing which child is behind, in which strand, in which region, into the Ministry's hands a full academic year sooner.

07 — Risks

What could stop this working

Principal risks and how each is answered
RiskSeverityResponse
Year-1 cash flow — introduced by the acceleration High The five-year total is unchanged, but year 1 rises from GY$266M to GY$481M. This is the binding constraint on the accelerated plan: if the Ministry cannot appropriate GY$481M in the first year, the schedule cannot hold no matter what the five-year figure says. Fallback is a hybrid — accelerate the pilot and primary core, let phases 3 and 4 run to the base timetable.
Less time between pilot and scale — introduced by the acceleration High The base plan left nine months between pilot end and national primary completion; the accelerated plan leaves five. If the pilot surfaces something structural there is less room to fix it before scale. Mitigated by doubling the pilot to forty schools and four districts, so problems surface in more conditions and sooner within the window.
Reserve held flat while risk rises — the weakest part of this plan Medium The reserve stays at 12% and US$804,000 in total, unchanged from the base plan, while the delivery it covers is compressed and therefore riskier. We would rather carry 15% across years 1 and 2 — about US$140,000 more. Held at 12% only because the instruction was to keep the total fixed; if it is exceeded, the overrun comes out of year 4 and year 5 steady state, which is a real reduction in support, not a free buffer.
Connectivity and power in hinterland schools High Offline-first by design, not as a fallback. The plan engine runs entirely on-device; a school can operate a full term with no link. Content packs ship with the app.
Teacher capacity and trust High Cascade training with serving Guyanese teachers as master trainers; a district coordinator each; the plan always shows the evidence behind every goal so a teacher can disagree with it.
Device access Medium Designed for shared and low-end Android devices, one per teacher rather than one per pupil. Rides the Ministry's existing smart-classroom and Whole-School Network programmes rather than duplicating them.
Children's data protection High DPIA before any pupil record moves; data residency agreed with the Ministry; no email identities for children; access audit log; defined retention and deletion.
Curriculum drift from NCERD Medium NCERD owns the strands and objectives; the platform loads them as versioned content, and every generated item is reviewed by a Guyanese teacher before it reaches a child.
Over-reliance on the model Medium The plan engine is deterministic and testable — no model decides a baseline, a goal or a mastery judgement. AI writes practice items and tutors; it does not assess.
Sustainability after the contract Medium Handover begins in year 4, not year 5. Ministry staff are embedded in the team from year 2, and the running cost after handover is the US$160,000 technology line, not the programme cost.
Pilot fails to show a lift Accepted Then the Ministry does not proceed. The phase structure exists so that this decision costs the pilot and its wind-down rather than the full programme. Acceleration raises that exposure: the decision now sits at month 7 inside a US$2.30M year, so the wider forty-school pilot matters as much for the evidence as for the speed.
08 — What we need

Six things from the Ministry to begin

  • The NCERD curriculum guides, with official objective numbering. The platform's strands already follow the published guides; the official codes replace our internal references without any strand changing.
  • A school census extract — schools, rosters and staff — as the identity source of truth, refreshed each term.
  • A data-sharing agreement and DPIA sign-off under the Data Protection Act 2023, before any pupil record is transferred.
  • Assessment results feeds for the Grade 2, Grade 4, Grade 9 and NGSA assessments, so baselines start from what the Ministry already knows rather than from a fresh diagnostic.
  • A named counterpart in each of the eleven education districts, to pair with our coordinators.
  • Per-school connectivity status from the NDMA, so we know which schools must run fully offline and plan the content-pack logistics accordingly.
09 — Basis

Assumptions and sources

Everything above rests on public figures and stated planning assumptions. Where a number is an estimate it is marked as one, and the Ministry's own data will replace it in Phase 0.

Figures taken from public sources

  • School counts — 471 nursery, 436 primary, 242 secondary and 6 special schools, from the Ministry's published statistics (2017, the most recent public digest). New construction since then means the real total is higher, which raises rollout cost but not per-pupil cost.
  • Connectivity — 1,033 schools connected, 385 nursery and 489 primary among them, with 365 hinterland and remote schools connected in the last two years and 158 over low-earth-orbit satellite.
  • Education budget — GY$183.6 billion allocated to the sector in Budget 2026; GY$85,000 per child in direct support including the “Because We Care” and uniform grants and the new transportation grant.
  • Structure and assessments — Nursery, Grades 1–6 and Forms 1–5 across eleven education districts, with the NGSA at Grade 6 and CSEC at Form 5.

Planning estimates, to be replaced with Ministry data

  • 200,000 learners — derived from the ~115,000 nursery and primary children in the national school feeding programme plus secondary enrolment, and cross-checked against the school-age share of population. Treat as ±10%.
  • 10,500 teachers — derived from published pupil–teacher ratios by level, not from a staff register.
  • Salary costs — blended Guyana-market rates for a locally-based team. A team staffed from overseas would cost roughly three times as much and would not build local capacity.
  • AI pricing — published Sonnet-class rates at the time of writing, with no volume discount assumed.

Explicitly excluded

  • Pupil and teacher devices. The costing assumes the Ministry's existing smart-classroom, Whole-School Network and device programmes supply the hardware. If devices are added to scope, budget roughly US$110 per rugged Android tablet at one per teacher — about US$1.15 million once, plus replacement at a five-year cycle.
  • Connectivity charges, which sit with the NDMA.
  • Any Ministry staff time, which is real but not billed here.
